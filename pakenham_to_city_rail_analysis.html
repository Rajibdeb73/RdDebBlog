Metro Tunnel · The Big Switch · What it means for the South-East

Pakenham to the city got a new tunnel. For many commuters, it also got longer.

On 1 February 2026 Melbourne's $13.5bn Metro Tunnel fully opened, permanently rerouting the Cranbourne and East Pakenham lines underground through the CBD. It is the biggest network change in 40 years — and on Victoria's busiest rail corridor, the winners and losers are already sharply drawn. This is the CivicLens read on who rides, what broke, and why the government chose this over a City Loop route or a direct airport line.

01 · The Ridership

Who actually rides Pakenham → City

Before judging the change, it helps to size the corridor. The Pakenham and Cranbourne lines share tracks from Dandenong inward and together form the single heaviest artery on Melbourne's metropolitan network — carrying more people than the entire Belgrave/Lilydale group.

Two rider types on one line

The corridor blends two very different journeys. Metropolitan commuters — from Pakenham, Officer, Beaconsfield, Berwick and the Casey growth suburbs — make the daily one-hour-plus haul into the CBD for work, study and events. On top of them sit V/Line Gippsland passengers from Traralgon, Morwell, Warragul and Drouin, whose regional trains share the same corridor into Southern Cross and Flinders Street.

That mix matters: a routing decision optimised for one group can quietly penalise the other. The Metro Tunnel switch is exactly such a decision — and it landed hardest on the outer-suburban metropolitan commuter with a destination in the old City Loop.

02 · What Changed

The "Big Switch": from City Loop to the Metro Tunnel

The change residents call "the new train underline pass" is the Metro Tunnel — a twin 9km bore under the CBD with five new underground stations. Cranbourne and East Pakenham services no longer run into the City Loop. They dive into the tunnel and continue north-west to become the Sunbury line.

03 · The Challenges

What commuters are struggling with

Within days of the full switch, local Facebook groups and Star News mastheads filled with the same complaints. CivicLens has grouped them by severity. The common thread: a network optimised for throughput removed the one-seat ride that most outer-suburban riders actually depended on.

● Critical

Lost direct City Loop & Richmond access

Cranbourne/East Pakenham trains no longer serve Richmond, South Yarra or the City Loop. To reach them, passengers must change at Caulfield or backtrack from Town Hall — a structural loss of the one-seat ride the majority relied on.

● Critical

Longer journeys, forced interchange

A Beaconsfield→Southern Cross trip rose from ~1h07 to 1h20–1h30. Cranbourne→Richmond went from ~53 min to ~1h15 via a Malvern change. The CRRA reports 20–40 extra minutes a day for affected riders.

▲ High

Accessibility & interchange burden

Transfers at Caulfield mean disembarking, crossing via the underpass and climbing to another platform on tight connections. Town Hall adds a ~280m walk and two flights of escalators — hard for people with mobility issues, prams or luggage.

▲ High

Events & MCG cut off

The sports and events precinct at Richmond — footy, cricket, tennis, concerts — sits on the south-east's own side of the city, yet now requires an interchange. A frequent, visible pain point for families.

◆ Moderate

Crowding at transfer nodes

Riders report "piling off" at Caulfield and Town Hall onto stopping trains, concentrating crowds and dwell time at the new interchange points during peak.

◆ Moderate

Cold-weather waits & wayfinding

Short, mistimed connections have left passengers waiting up to 20 minutes for the "connecting" service, with confusion over new transfer points adding stress to an already long commute.

It isn't all loss — the winners

CivicLens is careful not to flatten the picture. The CRRA's own framing is "winners and losers." Genuine winners are riders bound for the new tunnel stations: the Parkville hospital-and-university precinct, State Library / RMIT, and Town Hall for the eastern CBD — destinations that never had rail before. For them trips are more direct, more reliable, and far more frequent.

The losers are the larger group whose destination was the old City Loop, Richmond or Southern Cross for onward V/Line and interstate travel. The switch traded their one-seat convenience for network-wide capacity. Whether that trade is fair is now a live political question.

04 · Commuter Voices & the Petition

The community pushback

A parliamentary petition — launched by Beaconsfield commuter Kylie Robinson and sponsored by Berwick MP Brad Battin — had gathered more than 700 signatures by mid-2026, seeking to restore City Loop and Richmond access. It remains open until 31 August 2026 before being tabled for a government response.

My experience is just that it takes me 13 to 23 minutes longer. I'm stopping at more stations. I have to walk 280 metres to transit at Town Hall… For somebody with a disability, it's not even direct.

Kylie Robinson · Beaconsfield commuter, petition organiser

Right now, the pain is falling most heavily on south-east commuters who've lost their one-seat ride into the City Loop and key inner-city stations… it's adding 20 to 40 minutes a day.

Anthony Tassone · President, Casey Residents & Ratepayers Association

We were required to disembark at Caulfield… sprint through the gates, travel through the underpass, and return up to another platform to make our supposed 'connecting' City Loop train. On our return we waited a further 20 minutes in cold weather.

Nicole Edgar · Narre Warren South — proposes 2-in-3 trains keep the City Loop route

The government's answer

The Department of Transport and Planning responds that the City Loop was full: "Because we built the Metro Tunnel, we've delivered 1,200 extra weekly services on the Cranbourne, Pakenham and Sunbury lines, while creating room for more trains right across Melbourne's rail network."

Its advice to affected riders: interchange to City Loop and Richmond services at connected stations or transfer at Caulfield. On this reading, the individual inconvenience is the price of untangling the network's busiest bottleneck.

05 · V/Line Gippsland

What it means for regional passengers

Gippsland's V/Line trains share the Pakenham corridor, so residents naturally ask whether they too were rerouted. The answer, per the Minister for Transport Infrastructure: no. There are no current plans to transfer Gippsland passengers onto metropolitan services at Pakenham East. V/Line Gippsland trains continue to originate and terminate at Southern Cross or Flinders Street — the very stations the metro switch moved away from.

The net effect is subtle. Gippsland riders keep their direct city terminals, and gain easier access to the new Metro Tunnel precincts by interchanging: a trip to Victoria University in Footscray, for example, drops from three services to two. But it also means the region's mainline and the metro corridor now diverge in their CBD logic — one still loops to the traditional termini, the other burrows through the tunnel.

  • 🚆 Unchanged termini — Southern Cross / Flinders Street
  • 🔁 No forced metro transfer at Pakenham East
  • ⏱️ Footscray in 2 services, not 3 via tunnel interchange
  • 🏥 New reach to Parkville precinct through connections
06 · The Rationale

Why the government went for the tunnel

The recurring resident question is "why change something that worked?" The engineering answer is that it didn't work at scale — the City Loop had run out of room, and no amount of extra Pakenham/Cranbourne trains could be added without stealing paths from other lines.

1

The City Loop was full

The four-track loop, opened in the 1980s, had reached capacity. On the busiest corridor in Melbourne, the only way to add services was to stop feeding trains into a saturated loop — which is precisely what the tunnel does.

2

A through-running "spine" beats a terminating loop

By joining Cranbourne/Pakenham in the south-east to Sunbury in the north-west, trains pass through the CBD instead of terminating in it. That frees platform capacity at Flinders Street and Southern Cross and lets trains run as often as every 2–3 minutes.

3

New destinations that never had rail

The tunnel deliberately routes under the Parkville biomedical precinct and the education spine — connecting Victorians to hospitals, universities and jobs "never before accessed by rail," in the DTP's words.

4

Capacity released across the whole network

Because three lines left the loop, paths opened for other lines to run more often. The benefit is network-wide and largely invisible to the individual Pakenham rider who only feels their own longer trip.

The CivicLens caveat

The logic is sound at the system level, but it externalised a real cost onto a specific, identifiable group: outer south-east commuters with City Loop or Richmond destinations. Nicole Edgar's "2-in-3 trains keep the loop" proposal shows a middle path was conceivable. The unanswered governance question is whether that trade-off was adequately consulted, communicated and mitigated — or simply engineered and announced.

07 · The Airport Question

Why not a direct line to Melbourne Airport?

Residents often pose the tunnel decision against the airport rail that Melbourne has waited on for over 50 years: why pour billions into a CBD tunnel and a Sunshine "superhub" instead of a direct, express airport connection? The two projects are, in fact, deliberately joined — and the answer reveals the government's whole network philosophy.

The airport line routes via Sunshine — on purpose

Melbourne Airport Rail will run the airport into the CBD via Sunshine and Footscray, through the same Metro Tunnel, reaching Town Hall in around 30 minutes. Rather than a dedicated express, Sunshine becomes a "superhub" interchange where airport, metro and regional trains meet.

The stated rationale: pre-COVID, more than 40% of the 20,000+ daily trips to the airport came from Ballarat, Bendigo and Geelong. A Sunshine route serves those regional Victorians — not just CBD-bound flyers — and unlocks the Western Rail Plan and future Melton electrification. The government's line: it "benefits most Victorians."

Why there's still no train — the delays

  • 2018 — announced, targeting 2029 completion
  • 2023 — paused amid an above-vs-below-ground station dispute with Melbourne Airport
  • Mid-2024 — resolved; airport agrees to an elevated station
  • 2026 — construction restarts; Sunshine superhub build begins
  • ~2030 — Stage 1 (West Footscray–Albion rail untangling) due to complete first

The connection Pakenham residents should see

Here is the point the two debates share. A "direct Pakenham-to-Airport" line was never on the table because Melbourne isn't being built as a set of point-to-point express lines. It is being built around one shared CBD spine — the Metro Tunnel — fed from both ends: the south-east (Pakenham/Cranbourne) in, the north-west (Sunbury, and later the airport and regional lines via Sunshine) out.

That is why Pakenham commuters lost their one-seat ride, and why airport passengers will interchange at Sunshine: the network's design bet is on high-frequency corridors with hub interchanges, not on preserving every direct journey. Whether that bet serves outer-suburban residents as well as it serves the system is the question CivicLens will keep asking.

CivicLens Verdict

A network-level win that quietly billed an outer-suburban minority for the upgrade.

The Metro Tunnel is a genuine generational advance: it untangles Melbourne's busiest corridor, adds capacity across the whole network, and finally puts the Parkville health and education precinct on the rail map. On the numbers, the government's case holds.

But on the busiest corridor in the state, the switch removed the one-seat ride to the City Loop, Richmond and Southern Cross that most Pakenham and Cranbourne riders actually used — adding 13 to 40 minutes a day, an interchange, a 280-metre walk and two flights of escalators. The airport-rail decision follows the same philosophy: shared tunnel spine, hub interchange at Sunshine, no direct express. It is coherent system design. The open question is governance — whether the trade-off was consulted and mitigated, or engineered and announced — and whether a mixed service pattern could still soften the blow for the outer south-east.
